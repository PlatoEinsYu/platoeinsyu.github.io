Tate's Thesis used an adelic method to deal with functional equations of different types of L-functions, where the character could be made into Riemann-Zeta L-functions, Dirichlet L-functions and Hecke L-functions. He mainly considered about global and local eigendistributions, getting the basis of Schwartz-Bruhat functions and the corresponding distribution space, where global and local L-factors serves as coefficients of different basis, i.e.

$$
    z_0(s,\omega) L_v(s,\omega) = z(s,\omega).
$$

for local eigendistributions.

Using the Fourier transformation, we get two basis in the one-dim eigendistribution, which induces an functional equation:

$$
    \widehat{z_0(1-s,\omega^{-1})} = \epsilon_v(s,\omega,\psi) z_0(s,\omega).
$$

where $\psi$ is for the Fourier transformation. $v$ is a (local) place. Actually we can calculate all $\epsilon_v$ factors at $v$ whenever $\omega,\psi$ have different ramification properties or being archimedean.

However, globally, using Poisson summation and Riemann-Roch theorem, we can also get an global functional equation for $z(s,\omega)$.

$$
    z(s,\omega) = \widehat{z(1-s,\omega^{-1})}.
$$

Defining the global L-function to be $$\Lambda(s,\omega) = \prod_{v} L_v(s,\omega)$$, and combine all coefficients together, we get the final functional equation:

$$
    \Lambda(s,\omega) = \epsilon(s,\omega)\cdot \Lambda(1-s,\omega^{-1}).
$$

Where all equations are on where those functions are defined, and those functions have meromorphic continuation to the whole plane, where reader can refer to:
